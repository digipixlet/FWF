The strongest stakeholder video should not begin with the technology. It should begin with the place, the challenges facing that place and the consequences of leaving those challenges unresolved.

Purpose of this document
This document sets out a first-pass narrative and storyboard framework for a proposed Fishwater Flats stakeholder video. Its purpose is to test the proposed story, sequence and key stakeholder messages before the development of a final script and production brief.

The technical, regulatory, environmental and commercial statements contained in this draft remain subject to confirmation by the relevant members of the Project Team before production.

The central story is:

Nelson Mandela Bay faces three connected challenges: the responsible management of municipal sludge and organic waste; environmental and infrastructure pressure associated with the Fishwater Flats and Swartkops system; and growing demand from local industries for reliable, lower-carbon energy. The Fishwater Flats Biogas Project creates an integrated platform capable of responding to all three.

The Project information currently available provides the core facts required to support this narrative. The Fishwater Flats Wastewater Treatment Works receives approximately 155 ML of wastewater per day and generates the equivalent of approximately 74 tonnes of dry sludge matter per day, subject to final technical confirmation. At the same time, local manufacturers remain dependent on fossil fuels and are increasingly exposed to carbon taxes, emissions-reporting requirements and pressure to decarbonise their operations.

Phase 2 is intended to combine municipal sludge with controlled, source-separated organic wastes and convert those feedstocks into biomethane, renewable power and purified liquid CO₂, with the potential for additional environmental and agricultural value from carbon credits and processed digestate.

Proposed video format for discussion

I suggest producing a primary stakeholder film of approximately 8–9 minutes, structured so that shorter versions can later be cut from it:
90-second introductory film for general stakeholders;
3-minute regulator and municipal version;
3-minute investor version;
technical modules for partners, EPC contractors and suppliers;
individual explainer clips for biomethane, CO₂, feedstock, digestion and logistics.

The primary film should feel like a documentary-style infrastructure explainer rather than an investment presentation converted into video. Its first purpose should be to explain the Project clearly and credibly. Investor-specific financial information can then be incorporated into a separate, targeted version.

Important narrative disciplines

1. Keep the primary film explanatory and broadly stakeholder-neutral

The primary film should not feel as though it is primarily asking the audience for funding. It should first establish confidence in the problem definition, the Project logic, the technical process, the implementation status and the stakeholder benefits.

The detailed funding requirement, capital structure and projected investor returns should be presented in a separate investor-specific version.


2. Distinguish completed, contracted and future elements consistently

The film should carefully distinguish between:
approvals already obtained;
contracts executed;
term sheets signed;
agreements under negotiation;
future opportunities.

For example, the memorandum describes Puregas as having an executed term sheet, Borbet as being in GSA negotiations, and Volkswagen and Swartkops opportunities at earlier commercial stages. These should not all be presented as equally contracted.

A useful visual convention would be:
Completed — approval, work or milestone fully achieved
Contracted — binding agreement executed
In finalisation — substantive terms agreed but final documentation outstanding
In development — active technical or commercial engagement underway
Future expansion — potential opportunity not included in the current core case

The same classifications should be used throughout the film, maps, graphics and supporting documents.


3. Verify all counterparties and disclosure rights

No customer, supplier, funder, technology provider or municipal counterparty should be named, shown or represented by logo unless the current commercial status and disclosure rights have been confirmed. Where approval has not been obtained, the film should use a generic description such as “a nearby automotive component manufacturer” or “a prospective industrial offtaker”.


4. Avoid excessive jargon

Terms such as CSTR, PSA, HRT, biomethane, digestate and take-or-pay can be used, but each should be explained visually and in plain language when first introduced.


5. Build safety and operational continuity into the story

Safety should not appear only as a regulator-facing footnote. The film should show that gas handling, pipeline operation, compression, cryogenic storage, tanker loading, process monitoring and emergency response are integral to the design and operating philosophy.

It should also demonstrate that the Project will be implemented through clearly defined interfaces with the existing wastewater treatment works.


6. Show physical movement

The strongest visual device will be the movement of material and energy:

sludge and organics → digestion → raw biogas → biomethane and CO₂ → industry, transport and power.

That “journey” should be the visual spine of the film.


7. Position the project as infrastructure, not merely equipment

The enduring message should be:


This is an integrated municipal, environmental and industrial platform.

The digesters are central, but they are only one part of the story. The actual project includes feedstock agreements, receiving systems, gas upgrading, liquefaction, storage, compression, pipelines, logistics, power, O&M and long-term commercial arrangements.
